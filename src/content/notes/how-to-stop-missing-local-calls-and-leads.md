It's a Thursday afternoon at a made-up small engine shop out past the feed store. We'll call it Juniper Ridge Small Engine. Walt has a mower deck in pieces on the bench and grease up to his wrists. His phone buzzes in his back pocket, rings a few times, and stops.

That was someone with a chainsaw that won't start and a free Saturday. They didn't leave a message. They tapped the next shop on the list.

Walt never hears about it. Missed calls don't complain. They just go quiet and go somewhere else.

A lot of owners think the problem is getting found. Sometimes it is. But sometimes people already found you. They tried to reach you, and the path broke. That isn't a marketing problem. It's a leak, and the fixes below cost little or nothing.

## Can a small shop really never miss a business call?

Honestly, no. You're on a ladder, under a truck, or helping the customer standing right in front of you. Some calls will go to voicemail, and that's fine.

The real goal is smaller and a lot more doable: nobody who calls you gets left with nothing. Every caller either reaches a person, or hears a clear message and gets a call back when you said they would. That's what "never miss a business call" looks like for a real shop.

## Step 1: Walk the path a customer takes

Spend one afternoon pretending you're a new customer. Use your phone, not the shop computer.

1. Search what you do and your town on Google. Find your listing.
2. Tap the Call button on your listing. Who picks up? How many rings before voicemail?
3. Try the number on your website. Is it the same number, or a leftover one?
4. Send a test message through your contact form. Where does it land, and when?
5. Call after hours. What does someone hear at 7 PM?

Write down every dead end. If the Google listing itself looks forgotten, [start with the three misses we see most](/notes/3-things-most-small-rural-businesses-get-wrong-on-google).

## Step 2: Set up the phone so it can't quietly fail

You don't need a new phone system. You need the one you have, set up on purpose.

- **One public number.** Pick the number you want customers to call, and put that same number everywhere: Google, your website, Facebook, the truck, the business card.
- **Voicemail that's actually on.** Call it yourself. Make sure it's set up, it isn't full, and the greeting is yours. A full mailbox tells a caller nobody's home.
- **Enough rings.** If voicemail grabs the call after a ring or two, you'll miss people you could have caught. Ask your phone company whether you can give it a few more.
- **A ringer you'll hear.** If the shop is loud, keep the phone on you, not in the truck. Make sure a silent or do not disturb setting isn't left on during open hours.
- **Missed calls you'll see.** Know where your phone shows missed calls and voicemails, and look there on purpose.

## Step 3: Record a voicemail greeting that sets the clock

A good greeting answers three questions. Did I reach the right place? When will I hear back? What should I say?

Here's one you can borrow:

> "You've reached Juniper Ridge Small Engine. We're probably out in the shop. Leave your name, your number, and what you need fixed, and we'll call you back by the end of the day. If it's after 5, we'll call you first thing in the morning."

Keep it short and true. If you only return calls in the morning, say so. People will wait for honesty. They won't wait for silence.

Update it when things change: a holiday, a week away, a new number. A greeting that promises a call back you won't make is worse than no promise at all.

## Step 4: Build a callback habit

This is the part that actually closes the leak.

- **Pick two callback times a day.** Say, before lunch and before you lock up. Set them as reminders on your phone.
- **Call back missed numbers, even with no message.** Some people hang up on voicemail. A quick "Hey, this is Walt at Juniper Ridge, did you just call?" is fair game.
- **A short text counts.** If you can't talk, "Got your call, I'm in the middle of a job, I'll call you after 3" buys you time and goodwill.
- **Write it down.** A notebook by the register works fine. Name, number, what they need, and whether you called back.

The same goes for your contact form and email. That message has to land somewhere you'll see it, not a forgotten Gmail or an old "sales@" address. Keep the form short (name, how to reach them, what they need, maybe the town), and say on the page when you read them: "We read these on weekday mornings."

## Step 5: Make your Google listing match the phone

When someone finds you on Google on their phone, the Call button is right there. So make sure it rings the right phone.

- Sign in with the Google account that manages your listing, choose **Edit profile**, and check the phone number. It should be your one public number. Not sure which account holds the keys? Here's how to check [who set up your Google listing](/notes/who-set-up-your-google-listing).
- In the same place, check your **Hours**. Your hours tell people when it's okay to call. If Google says you're open till 6 and nobody answers at 5:30, that call feels like a locked door. If your hours are off, here's [what to do when Google shows inaccurate business hours](/notes/what-to-do-when-google-shows-the-wrong-hours).
- If you mostly work by callback, say that plainly on your website and in your voicemail, so nobody has to guess.

## Step 6: Stop making them hunt

- Put the phone number where a thumb can tap it on your website, near the top and again in the footer.
- Match the name and phone number across Google, your website, and Facebook.
- If you serve an area instead of running a storefront, say which towns.

You don't need a funnel. You need [a presence that's easy to reach](/notes/what-a-clean-simple-online-presence-looks-like), and someone who picks up or calls back.

## A simple weekly check

Pick one day a week, the same day every week, and run through this:

1. Call your own public number from another phone. Does it ring, and does the greeting still fit?
2. Send yourself a test through the contact form.
3. Look through missed calls and messages. Did everyone get a reply?
4. Open your Google listing on your phone. Right number? Right hours?
5. Clear out old voicemails so the box never fills up.

That's the whole system. No dashboard required.

## One thing to do this week

Call your own shop number from a friend's phone and let it ring out. Listen to what a stranger hears. If it's a full mailbox, an old greeting, or nothing at all, fix that one thing first.
