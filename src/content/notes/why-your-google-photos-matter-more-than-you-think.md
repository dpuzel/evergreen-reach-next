When someone nearby looks you up, they do not start with your story. They glance at the hours, then they look at the pictures.

If the pictures look like this month, they assume the shop does too. If the pictures look like a different paint job, a truck you sold, or a stock kitchen that is not yours, they assume nobody is tending the porch. They do not leave a review about it. They just go somewhere that looks open.

This is not a photography problem. It is a trust problem. We already wrote about [the three misses we see most on Google](/notes/3-things-most-small-rural-businesses-get-wrong-on-google). Photos are the one people skip because the shop is busy, and the one a stranger uses to decide whether to drive over.

## What the pictures are actually doing

Your Google photos are doing three jobs at once.

They prove the place is real. A neighbor who has never been inside is trying to tell you apart from a name collision, a closed shop, or a listing that Google invented from old data.

They prove the place is current. Google will show how old a photo is. You do not have to say "we are still here." A picture from this season says it.

They prove the work looks like the reviews. If people talk about jalapeño cheddar, boat seats, or a nervous dog who left calm, and the grid is still a blurry door from 2019, the listing argues with itself.

A short description helps. Hours help. The phone helps. Pictures are what a thumb stops on.

## Alive is not the same as pretty

You do not need a photographer. You need pictures that look like the shop this month.

Alive looks like:

- The door a stranger will actually walk up to
- The work on the table, in the bay, or in the booth
- A finished job, still in the room where it happened
- The counter, the yard, the suite number, something a person would recognize when they arrive

Pretty-but-dead looks like:

- A stock bakery that is not yours
- A generated image with a filename nobody would type
- The old lot after you moved across town
- A hero shot of a truck you sold last year
- One logo, tiled six times

Customer photos will show up whether you like them or not. That is fine. They are one neighbor talking to another. Owner photos are the ones you control. If the owner grid is empty, the customer grid becomes the whole story.

If you do not have a storefront, do not fake one. A van, a kit, a job site, and the work itself are more honest than a pin on a building you do not staff.

## Four pictures cover most shops

When we walk a listing, we look for four.

1. The door. Storefront, suite, farm gate, or the side of the van. Whatever a first-time customer will match to the map.
2. The work in progress. Dough on the bench. A seat pulled. A dog on the table. A coach in the bay.
3. A finished job. The loaf, the interior, the groom, the coach leaving.
4. A human scale shot. The counter. The yard. The person who actually picks up.

That set does more than a gallery of twenty similar angles. It answers "is this real, is this current, and is this the place I am about to drive to."

Take them on your phone, in daylight, without a filter that lies. Do not photograph other people's kids, open receipts, or plates if you can help it. The work is enough.

## What to do this week

You do not need a content calendar. You need a pass.

1. Open your listing the way a stranger would, on a phone.
2. Scroll the photos. Write down anything that is not this shop, this year, or this address.
3. Hide or remove the ones that belong to a different door.
4. Add those four pictures from this week.
5. If a customer photo is the only current one, add an owner photo beside it so the grid is not only other people's phones.

Then once a month, add one more real picture when something actually happens. A seasonal special. A new sign. The morning counter. Consistency beats a launch week of twelve shots you never touch again.

## The listing should look like the shop

Hours, phone, and category still have to tell the truth. Pictures will not save a number that rings out, or a pin that sends people to the old suite. They will make a tended listing look tended.

If you want the whole walk, not just the camera roll, that is what a [Front Porch Report](/porch) is for. Six lamps. One of them is whether the photos still look like the place. A [clean, simple presence](/notes/what-a-clean-simple-online-presence-looks-like) is the rest of the house matching that porch.

The goal is small. When someone nearby looks you up, the first pictures they see should look like a shop that is open, proud, and paying attention this month. That is usually enough to get them to tap the number.
