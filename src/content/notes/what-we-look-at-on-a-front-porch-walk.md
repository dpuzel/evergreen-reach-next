Before anyone calls a local shop, they usually take a quick look first. They check the Google card, the hours, a few photos, and the website. A Front Porch walk is us taking that same look, slowly, and writing down what a neighbor would notice.

We don't log in to anything, and we don't need your passwords. We only look at what anyone can see from the street, so to speak. Then we put it on one plain page, the Front Porch Report, with six lamps. Each lamp is lit, low, or out, and each one comes with one next move.

Here's what each lamp means, and why we look at it.

## Found nearby: do you show up when someone searches?

We search for what you do, in your town, the way a neighbor would. Then we check whether your Google card comes up, whether its pin sits where your shop actually is, and whether its category says what you do.

What a neighbor notices: if you don't come up, they find someone else, and you never know they looked. We've seen a well-loved shop with no Google card at all, and another whose map pin sat miles away from the people recommending it.

What it tells you: whether your door is even on the map.

## Call-ready: does the number ring someone who can pick up?

We check that the same phone number shows up on your Google card, your website, and the other places people find you. We look for your email too, and whether a neighbor can actually see it.

What a neighbor notices: two different numbers, or a contact page with no way to reach you. We've seen a contact page hide the shop's email behind a blank label, while a flyer somewhere else printed it clearly.

What it tells you: whether a ready customer can reach you on the first try.

## Hours honest: do the hours on Google match the door?

We compare the hours on Google with the hours on your website and the directories that copy them.

What a neighbor notices: a locked door when Google said you were open, or "Closed" on a day you'd gladly have taken the call. We wrote a whole note on [what to do when Google shows the wrong hours](/notes/what-to-do-when-google-shows-the-wrong-hours).

What it tells you: whether a neighbor can trust the clock before they make the drive.

## Photos alive: do the pictures look like the place this month?

We look at the photos on your Google card and your site. Are there any? Are they recent? Do they show the outside, so people can find the door, and the real work you do?

What a neighbor notices: an empty photo box, or pictures from three owners ago. More on that in [why your Google photos matter more than you think](/notes/why-your-google-photos-matter-more-than-you-think).

What it tells you: whether a stranger can picture walking in.

## Site not rotting: does the website load, tell the truth, and belong to you?

We open your site and click around. We check that it loads, that the links go where they say, and that what it says is still true. We also look for small leftovers, like a page title still carrying a template name, or a social icon that goes nowhere.

What a neighbor notices: a broken link or a special that ended last spring reads as nobody home. We wrote about that in [the quiet damage of a dead social link](/notes/the-quiet-damage-of-a-dead-social-link).

What it tells you: whether your own front window is clean.

## Name, address, phone: does everything say the same thing?

Last, we line up your name, address, and phone everywhere we found you. That means Google, your site, MapQuest, Yelp, Nextdoor, and the old directories that copy each other.

What a neighbor notices: an old business name on a map card, or an address missing its suite number. Small mismatches make people wonder whether they have the right place.

What it tells you: whether the internet is telling one story about you, and whether it's the true one.

## What we don't do on a walk

We don't give you a score out of a hundred. We don't count your reviews or rank you against the shop down the road. And we don't promise that fixing a lamp brings in more calls. We just write down what we saw, plainly, with one next move for each lamp. Many of those next moves are things you can do yourself in an afternoon.

## How to take your own walk

You can do a rough version of this in about ten minutes. On your phone, search for what you do in your town. Find your card. Check the hours against your door, look at the photos, tap the phone number, and open your website. Then look your shop up on MapQuest or Yelp and see if the story matches.

Whatever you find, you'll know more than you did when you started.
